# Decks, Presentations & Self-Contained Sheets

The deck is the collateral format most often built wrong, because it *looks* easy — a title box and a bullet placeholder on every slide. That default is exactly the assembled-vs-designed failure from `design-principles`, thirty slides deep. This is how to design it instead.

## Decide the Mode First: Presented or Read

Before a single slide, answer: **will a person present this live, or will it be sent and read alone?** They are two different artifacts, and the most common deck failure is building one as the other.

- **Presented (talk-track).** The presenter carries the words. Slides are **sparse** — one idea, a big visual, a few words of anchor. The audience listens to a person and glances at a slide; a slide full of text competes with the speaker and loses (death by bullet — people read ahead and stop listening). Think: one bold statement, one image, one number.
- **Read (leave-behind / send).** No narrator. Every slide must **stand on its own** — the point, the support, and enough context to be understood cold. Denser is correct here; a one-word slide is useless in an inbox. This is closer to a document than a talk.

If the deck must do both (a pitch you present *and* email), the honest answer is usually **two versions or a hybrid** — a presented deck with a fuller appendix, or speaker-note density that a reader can fall back on. Decide it; don't split the difference by accident and ship a deck that's too sparse to read and too busy to present.

## The Deck Is a Sequence, Not a Stack

A deck has an **arc**. Each slide earns its place in a narrative — setup, tension, turn, resolution — the same way a page has a reading order. Design the *flow*, not 20 independent canvases:

- **One idea per slide.** If a slide has two ideas, it's two slides. If it has five bullets, it's usually one idea buried under four — find it and cut the rest, or split it out.
- **Rhythm across the deck.** A shared master holds the deck together; **varied layouts** keep it alive. Alternate full-bleed statement slides, split image/text, a big-number slide, a quote slide, a data slide, a section divider. Twenty identical title-and-body slides is the deck version of banded web sections — coherent and dead.
- **Section dividers signal structure.** A repeated divider treatment says "new chapter"; it's the deck's version of "repeat a pattern to signal structure, break it to signal difference."

## Slide Anatomy

- **A master, not a per-slide guess.** Fixed margins, a safe title zone, a consistent footer (mark + page number where useful). Everything lives on a grid so the deck feels engineered, not hand-placed slide by slide.
- **Hierarchy per slide.** Name the one thing that matters on this slide; make its size, position, and surrounding space match that rank. The slide title is not automatically the biggest thing — often the *visual* or the *number* is, and the title is a small kicker.
- **Kill bullet soup.** Bullets are the enemy of hierarchy — they flatten everything to one weight. Replace a bullet list with: one statement + a visual; a 2×2; a stepped build; or a small number of *ranked* points where the primary one is scaled up. If you must list, make the list earn it (short, parallel, scannable) and never nest.
- **Type scaled for the room.** A presented slide is read from the back; size type decisively larger than a document. Body that looks fine on your laptop is illegible projected — test it.
- **Contrast for the projector.** Bright rooms crush subtle low-contrast pairs. A tint-on-tint that reads on your monitor vanishes on a projector. Give text real contrast against its ground.

## Data Slides

- **One takeaway, and the takeaway is the title.** Not "Q3 Revenue" — "Revenue doubled after launch." The chart proves the sentence; the sentence tells the reader what to see. Everything else on the slide supports that one read.
- **Style the chart to the system.** Use `dataviz` so color, type, and marks match the brand and read as one system across every data slide. No default Excel palette; no rainbow categorical when two series would do.
- **Reduce the chart to its point.** Strip gridlines, redundant labels, and legend chrome that the takeaway already carries. A data slide is a sentence with evidence, not a spreadsheet on screen.

## Self-Contained Sheets (One-Pagers, Sales Sheets, Leave-Behinds)

A one-pager is a *read* artifact with **no scroll and no presenter** — everything on one frame, working the instant it's picked up. The discipline:

- **One primary message, ranked proof beneath it.** It has to answer "what is this and why should I care" in the first glance, then reward a second read with the support. Dense is fine; **flat is the failure** — a wall of equal-weight paragraphs has no entry point.
- **A clear entry point and reading path.** The eye should land on the headline/hero, then follow an intended route through the proof. Left-align by default; a fixed frame tempts you to center everything, which drifts off the grid and kills the path.
- **Balance text with a visual.** A text-only sheet that should carry a product shot, diagram, or key stat reads as unfinished — the same rule as a web section.
- **It stands alone.** No "as I mentioned," no missing context. If it's a sales sheet, the substance (what to claim, the proof points, the battle-card content) comes from `sales-enablement` / `copywriting`; you rank and stage it.

## Common Deck Failures (feed these into the slop check)

- Every slide a title + three bullets (the template underneath, undisguised)
- A read-deck built as one-word talk-track slides — useless when emailed
- A talk-track deck with paragraph slides — the audience reads and stops listening
- Data slides titled with the metric ("Q3 Revenue") instead of the takeaway
- Twenty identical layouts — coherent, lifeless, no rhythm
- Under-scaled type and low-contrast pairs that die on a projector
- The logo huge on every slide instead of a quiet footer
