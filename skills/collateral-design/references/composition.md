# Composing a Fixed Format — Dynamic, Not Filled

A fixed frame is unforgiving: there's no scroll to rescue a flat layout and no interaction to add interest. Composition is most of the craft here. Three things separate a dynamic piece from a filled template: **light-first thinking**, a **background element that ties the composition together**, and a **grid fine enough to build varied layouts on**.

## Light First, Then Dark

**Default to a light composition and design the dark variant second** — especially for anything printed. A near-black printed piece is rare and reads as odd: it drinks ink, it shows every scuff and fingerprint, it costs more, and on uncoated stock it goes muddy. Most printed collateral is light.

- **Design the light expression first**, even when the brand's screen identity is dark. A dark brand still needs a light expression to exist on paper — warm paper/off-white ground, near-black ink for text, the accent darkened enough to hold contrast against light (a bright screen amber often needs a deeper cut to stay legible as text on cream).
- **Then produce the dark variant** for screen surfaces where it belongs (social, digital PDFs, presented decks in a dark room).
- **If a piece genuinely should be dark, give it depth** — a rich gradient with light in it, not flat black. A navy→teal or warm-black→ember wash with a soft glow reads as designed; a flat `#000` field reads as an absence of a decision.
- Deliver **both** where it matters, so the brand has a light *and* a dark expression on record.

## A Background Element Ties the Composition Together

The most common reason a fixed-format piece feels flat: it's text and boxes on an empty ground. **You need a background element that moves through the composition and connects its parts** — this is what makes a layout feel composed rather than filled.

Reach for:

- **A large gradient wash** anchored to a corner or bleeding off an edge — warm light in the top-right, a colored glow rising from the bottom.
- **Big patterned elements** — an oversized version of the brand's motif, an isometric field, a dot/grid texture, concentric arcs — scaled up large and cropped by the frame, sitting *behind* the content.
- **Graphic lines or shapes crossing the composition** — a curve, a diagonal, a chart-line overlaying a photograph, a connecting rule threading numbered steps together.
- **Oversized brand marks or numerals**, cropped by the trim, used as texture rather than as a logo.

Rules that keep it from becoming noise: the background element sits behind and **never competes with the headline or the action**; it's usually low-contrast against the ground; and it should feel *cropped by the frame* (running off the edge) rather than politely contained. On print, background art that touches an edge **must bleed** — see [print.md](print.md).

## Build on a Fine Grid — Not a Two-Column Repeat

A two-column grid repeated down the whole piece is the fixed-format equivalent of banded web sections: coherent, safe, and dull. **Set up an 8-column grid** (12 also works) and compose *across* it — the same discipline `web-design` applies to sections, applied to a page or a slide.

- Vary the band proportions: a 5+3 hero, a 4+4 pair, a full-8 statement, a 3+3+2, a 6+2 with a narrow sidebar. The frame gets a **rhythm** instead of one repeated split.
- Let a column count change the *pace*: dense multi-column detail after a wide open statement reads as deliberate.
- Keep the gutters and the outer margin consistent even as the spans change — that's what holds it together while the layout varies.
- **Mix the media across bands**, not just the columns: photography, vector, iconography, a device mockup, a chart, a pull-quote card. A page where every band is text-in-boxes is the flat case even if the spans differ.

Composition patterns worth stealing (all visible in strong reference collateral): a key phrase in the headline **underlined or highlighted** in the accent; one **filled/inverted card** among outlined ones to anchor a testimonial; **numbered steps threaded by a connecting rule**; a photo with a **graphic line overlay**; a light page **paired with a dark page** in a two-pager.

## Margin — In Print It's the Inside

Terminology matters because it inverts from web: in print, **margin is the space *inside* the trim**, between the edge and the content (what CSS calls padding). It is not an outside offset.

**Be generous with it — more than screen instincts suggest.** A typical screen-derived inset is roughly **a half to a third of what a print piece wants**. Ample interior margin is what gives a printed piece its air and its quality; cramped content reads cheap regardless of how good the type is. On a small format like a business card this is the single biggest lever — a card wants a genuinely large interior margin so the few elements on it can breathe.

Critical content also has to clear the **safe area** (~1/8in inside the trim), so treat safe as the hard floor and your design margin as considerably more than that. The exception is anything *intended* to run off the edge — a background wash, a cropped pattern, a bleeding photo — which ignores the margin and extends past trim into the bleed.
